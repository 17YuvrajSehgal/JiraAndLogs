We thank the reviewers for their careful feedback and for recognizing the relevance of the problem and the value of budgeted reasoning. The reviews identify a clear path to strengthen ARISE: evaluate the practical usefulness of retrieved incidents more directly, sharpen the interpretation of the real-data results, and expand the methodological and reproducibility details. We respectfully request the opportunity for a major revision to complete the concrete evaluations and improvements described below.
Reviewers A/B: Relevance labels and practical usefulness
The reported 0.970 Hit@5 on World of Logs measures retrieval under the stated relevance rule: the query and retrieved ticket belong to the same project and share at least one Jira component. The evaluation uses 5,150 eligible queries against 38,642 memory tickets. We will consistently identify this as retrieval under an inferred relevance relation, including in the abstract, and distinguish it from evidence of diagnostic or resolution usefulness.
Regarding overlap between the relevance definition and graph retrieval, the graph uses entities extracted from ticket text by a language model, rather than the Jira component field used to construct relevance labels. Graph-only retrieval achieves 0.308 Hit@5 under that same rule, and removing it from fusion produces no significant difference in the reported evaluation. These results help address the concern about direct use of labeling metadata, while leaving the broader question of practical usefulness to be evaluated independently.
Under the stricter relevance rule incorporating symptom overlap, hybrid retrieval achieves 0.74 Hit@5 (MRR 0.60) on the 4,362 of the 5,150 queries that retain at least one relevant ticket under that rule, with 0.80 on Kafka and 0.73 on MariaDB. We will report every retrieval baseline under both relevance definitions together with the eligible-query count for each, quantify missing component metadata and its effect on query eligibility, and assess sensitivity to missing or noisy metadata.
If given the opportunity for a major revision, our highest priority will be a blinded human evaluation of the actual top-ranked results returned by ARISE and the strongest baselines. Raters will assess whether retrieved incidents provide useful diagnostic or resolution information, using difficult alternatives from the same project, a documented rubric, and inter-rater agreement. This directly addresses the reviewers’ central question: whether the retrieved incidents help an engineer investigate the current issue.
The existing LLM assessment uses Qwen2.5-7B to rate 300 relevance-labeled pairs and 300 random pairs per dataset on a five-point scale. Its cross-project random controls and the low mean ratings it assigns to relevance-labeled pairs (2.3-2.6/5 across the three datasets) motivate the stronger evaluation above. We will present this assessment as exploratory and use the human study to evaluate practical usefulness.
Reviewers A/B: Real-data classification and paging interpretation
The telemetry-based classifiers expect numerical features that World of Logs does not carry. In the evaluated pipeline, all 94 feature columns were present but uniformly zero across all 78,140 rows, so the classifiers could only produce constant predictions. We will remove the claim that their chance-level performance shows that classification fails on real incidents and replace this invalid comparison with text-based classifiers trained on ticket descriptions under the same held-out-project partition.
Specifically, in the abstract and introduction, we will replace statements that ticket-worthiness classification “does not transfer” with a narrower description of the text-based, resolution-label prediction task and its measured results. In RQ4 and the discussion, we will replace the synthetic-versus-real classifier comparison with the text-based baseline results and state that the original zero-filled comparison cannot support a transfer conclusion.
ARISE’s reported 0.506 triage accuracy is a separate result, derived from retrieval-based scores over text-only inputs, so the zero-filled-feature explanation does not apply to it. We wish to be equally clear that this number is not evidence of effective classification. The test split is 50.6% ticket-worthy, so a constant predictor attains 0.506 as well; ARISE matches this majority-class baseline without exceeding it. Part of the shortfall is a label-space mismatch of our own construction: the controller emits a third “borderline” decision for suppressed pages on 3,061 of 13,388 windows, which the binary resolution-derived ground truth cannot score as correct. In the revision we will report the majority-class baseline alongside this number, correct the label-space mismatch, and state plainly that our own triage classification on real incidents is likewise at chance level. This does not undermine the paper’s reframing; it is further evidence that incident retrieval, rather than alert classification, is the target that transfers. We will make this distinction explicit in the experimental setup and RQ4 results.
Jira resolution status and operational page-worthiness are different concepts. We will rename the real-data target “resolution-derived labels” in the dataset description, results, and conclusion, and remove claims that these labels measure real-world paging effectiveness. Assessing page-worthiness would require independently annotated operational urgency, which this corpus does not provide.
Reviewers A/B: Scope of the real-world evidence
ARISE combines historical incident retrieval with budgeted evidence gathering that adapts to available inputs. The two telemetry datasets exercise the richer monitoring configuration. World of Logs evaluates the text-only configuration using real Jira descriptions, including embedded log excerpts, without aligned metrics, traces, or cluster events.
The real-data evaluation supports historical incident retrieval with text-only inputs: the full system achieves 0.963 Hit@5 under the stated relevance definition. As discussed in Section IX, it does not establish transfer of telemetry reasoning to production. We will align the abstract, contributions, and results discussion with this distinction and explicitly identify which evidence-gathering and novelty analyses are supported by each dataset.
Reviewer B: MAXCONF and controller thresholds
MAXCONF(R) takes the maximum of the available retrievers’ triage_score values, with zero as the default when no score is available. It is separate from the fused RRF ranking score. Thus, the 0.5 gate does not operate on the raw RRF score whose maximum the reviewer correctly computes as approximately 0.049.
For the fine-tuned dense retriever, this score is produced by a logistic head over similarity features. Hybrid retrieval likewise computes its triage score separately from the fused ranking. We will define the features, training procedure, score range, and any similarity-based fallback for each evaluated configuration in Section IV-A and Algorithm 1. This will distinguish learned probabilities from match-strength scores and make the scale of each controller decision explicit.
The controller thresholds of 0.90 and 0.5 were fixed in advance, rather than tuned on the reported test results. We will add sensitivity analyses for retrieval quality, escalation frequency, and cost. We will also distinguish the initial telemetry-based gate from subsequent retrieval-based decisions and align the algorithm, implementation description, and evaluated controller behavior.
Reviewer B: Cost estimates and measured execution
The reported 40–78% reductions are estimates obtained by applying a per-skill cost model to controller execution decisions. We will replace the wording in the Table III caption and Section VII-C, which currently present the reduction as an observed quantity in wall-clock time and model usage, with this model-based description, and report measured wall-clock time and token usage separately.
In the revision, we will report per-step invocation and skip counts, their denominators, and the cost assumptions. We will add measured runtime and model usage for comparable adaptive and run-all configurations, with offline training costs reported separately. This will let readers assess both the controller’s execution decisions and their computational benefit.
Reviewer C: Repeated runs and training protocol
Section VII-A already reports three-seed retriever results for the two telemetry datasets: 0.632 ± 0.015 and 0.678 ± 0.004. World of Logs was evaluated with one trained retriever. We will extend that experiment to multiple training seeds and report the resulting variability.
Retriever fine-tuning is performed offline, and the trained model is frozen during evaluation; it is not updated for each incident. We will make the training and inference stages explicit and add confidence intervals to Figure 4, distinguishing uncertainty across evaluated queries from variation across independent training runs. Fixed seeds and greedy decoding will be documented as reproducibility settings, alongside these repeated-run evaluations.
Reviewer C: Incident construction, labels, and presentation
Each memory item represents a resolved ticket: a historical Jira issue in World of Logs or a ticket generated for an injected fault in the telemetry datasets. Synthetic labels derive from known fault injections. Jira classification labels derive from issue type and resolution, while retrieval relevance is inferred using the project/component rule and its stricter variant. The LLM-judge assessment evaluates sampled pairs; it does not produce the benchmark’s relevance labels.
The current evaluation does not use human relevance judgments. We will replace the annotation-kit statement in Section IX with a precise description of the released labeling procedures and release the protocol and materials accompanying the proposed human study.
We will provide the complete judge prompt and sampling procedure, use one consistent name for “Hybrid-RRF” and “Hybrid fusion,” and rewrite the introduction passages identified by Reviewer C. Windows represent decisions over monitoring intervals; we will clarify how a continuous stream is grouped into these intervals and how timestamped historical memory is available to each decision.
Reviewer A: Related systems and contribution
We will expand the discussion of FLASH, Triangle, DeepCT, and COMET with an explicit comparison of objectives, required inputs, outputs, and evaluation settings. This will position ARISE’s contribution more precisely: combining historical incident retrieval with capability-aware, budgeted evidence gathering.
We will add an experimental comparison where the task and available inputs support a meaningful common evaluation, documenting any adaptations. For systems requiring different inputs or producing different outputs, the task-and-capability comparison will make those distinctions explicit.
Reviewer A: Artifact availability
The World of Logs names v1, v2, and v3 originated as internal names for progressively larger corpus variants. The reported experiments use v3, whereas the currently bundled dataset is v2. We will publish the exact evaluated v3 corpus in the cited Zenodo dataset record with a version identifier matching the manuscript, together with the corpus manifest, query and memory partitions, preprocessing instructions, and experiment configurations.
The existing retrieval results provide a foundation for ARISE’s contribution, and the proposed revisions directly address the reviewers’ questions about practical usefulness, comparison fairness, controller behavior, and reproducibility. We respectfully ask the committee to consider a major revision so that we can complete these evaluations and present the strengthened evidence.
