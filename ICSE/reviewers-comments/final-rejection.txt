Paper summary
-------------
The paper presents ARISE, an adaptive incident-triage framework that retrieves similar resolved incidents from an incident memory. The system combines dense retrieval, sparse lexical retrieval, and knowledge-graph retrieval, then uses capability gating to decide which retrieval or reasoning steps to run depending on available evidence and uncertainty. The evaluation uses three datasets: two newly collected telemetry-to-ticket datasets based on Online Boutique and OpenTelemetry Demo, and a real-world text-only corpus of 38,642 resolved Apache Jira incidents from 24 projects. The main result is that on the real corpus ARISE retrieves an inferred relevant prior incident in the top five 97% of the time, outperforming dense, lexical, graph, cross-encoder, and retrieval-augmented LLM baselines. Capability gating reduces per-window cost by up to 78% relative to a run-all cascade.

Strengths
---------
- The paper addresses an important and realistic operational problem: on-call engineers often rely on memory of prior incidents, and finding the right prior ticket is itself a hard retrieval problem.

- The evaluation includes two newly collected telemetry-to-ticket datasets plus a large real-world Jira corpus.

Weaknesses
----------
- The headline 97% Hit@5 result on real incidents depends on an inferred relevance relation, not manually validated ground truth. A query and prior ticket are considered relevant if they share project and component, with a stricter symptom-overlap variant. This relation may overestimate operational usefulness: two tickets can share project/component without being a genuinely helpful prior incident for on-call triage.

- The LLM-as-judge validation of inferred labels is not enough to remove the construct-validity concern. The judge compares inferred gold matches to random controls, which only shows that the inferred matches are less random than random tickets. It does not show that they are the right or useful incidents an engineer would retrieve.

- The real-world corpus is text-only, while the telemetry-rich datasets are synthetic. As a result, the paper cannot show that ARISE’s evidence-gathering and capability-gated telemetry reasoning transfer to real production telemetry. The strongest real-world result is essentially text-based incident retrieval.

- The triage-classification conclusion may be overstated. The paper shows that ticket-worthiness classification fails on text-only, resolution-derived Jira labels, but this does not necessarily imply that triage classification is generally a weak target. It may instead reflect noisy labels, missing telemetry, or mismatch between Jira resolution status and on-call ticket-worthiness.

- Limited comparison with related works.

Detailed comments for authors
-----------------------------
- The related-work discussion links incident-memory retrieval mainly to duplicate and similar bug-report retrieval. However, retrieving historical incidents has also been directly used in cloud incident management, for example to support root-cause analysis and to select candidate teams during incident triage. The paper should compare ARISE with these incident-level systems, or at least provide a clear task and capability comparison, rather than comparing only individual retrieval methods. For example:
 Yu et al. “Triangle: Empowering Incident Triage with Multi-Agent.” *ASE 2025*, pp. 674-686.

- The paper should be more careful in wording the 97% Hit@5 result. It is “97% under an inferred project-component relevance relation,” not necessarily 97% retrieval of genuinely useful prior incidents.

- The paper should report how often project/component metadata is missing, noisy, or inconsistent in World of Logs. Since the main real-corpus metric depends on these fields, their quality is load-bearing.

- The paper's real-world triage labels are derived from Jira resolution status, with fixed bugs treated as ticket-worthy and rejected/non-bug issues treated as noise. This creates a potential construct-validity problem: final resolution status may not correspond to on-call ticket-worthiness or page-worthiness. A rejected issue may still have required operational investigation, and a fixed issue may be low urgency. The authors should clarify this distinction and qualify the claim that triage classification collapses to chance in real operations.

Comments on artifact assessment
-------------------------------
Most of the artifacts declared in the paper are available. However, there are some issues—for example, the WOL-v3 mentioned in the paper is not in the link; only WOL-v2 is currently available.

Questions for authors’ response
-------------------------------
1. Can the authors provide human judgments showing that the inferred project–component matches are genuinely useful prior incidents, rather than merely more relevant than random controls?
2. Can the authors justify conclusions about capability-gated telemetry reasoning when the telemetry-rich datasets are synthetic and the real-world dataset is text-only?
3. Why is final Jira resolution an appropriate proxy for on-call ticket/page-worthiness, and would the triage conclusion hold under human-annotated page-worthiness labels, e.g., urgent ticket-worthy, valid but not urgent, or no immediate action?
4. Can the authors compare ARISE with incident-level systems that already retrieve similar historical incidents for RCA or team assignment (e.g., FLASH, Triangle, DeepCT, COMET), rather than only with individual retrieval methods, and clarify their differences in inputs, outputs, and evaluation?

Paper summary
-------------
This paper proposes ARISE, which retrieves similar resolved incidents to support on-call triage. It combines dense, sparse, and graph retrieval, then uses a deterministic controller to run expensive steps only when needed. It also suppresses repeated pages for the same incident. The evaluation uses two fault-injection datasets and an Apache Jira corpus with 38,642 resolved issues in retrieval memory. On Jira, hybrid retrieval reaches 0.970 Hit@5 under the paper's project-component relevance rule. The paper reports cost reductions of 40% to 78% against a run-all cascade.

Strengths
---------
- The problem is important to cloud incident mangement practice.
- The evaluation covers both telemetry-rich and text-only settings.
- The paper reports positive findings as well as negative ones.

Weaknesses
----------
- The main Jira result uses a relevance rule that overlaps with ARISE's retrieval features.
- The synthetic and real evaluations use different inputs and label definitions.
- The controller and cost evaluation lack important details.
- Novelty detection and evidence gathering are not fully evaluated on real data.

Detailed comments for authors
-----------------------------
### Rigor

An important problem is the Jira relevance definition. A past issue is considered relevant when it shares the query's project and at least one component. ARISE's graph retriever directly uses component overlap. The 0.970 Hit@5 result may therefore partly reflect the labeling rule, rather than finding incidents with a similar cause or useful resolution. The paper's nonsignificant graph ablation (Section VII-A) alleviates this concern but does not address its validity.

The stricter relevance rule also requires symptom overlap and yields a lower Hit@5 of 0.74, making the evaluation more meaningful. However, the graph retriever also uses symptom overlap, so some alignment between the method and the labels remains. The LLM validation uses random controls, which may be easy negatives. Stronger evidence would come from engineers evaluating the actual top-ranked results against difficult alternatives from the same project.

The synthetic-versus-real comparison is a bit overstated. The synthetic data consists of telemetry windows labeled by whether they should trigger a page, while the Jira data consists of filed issues labeled by their type and final resolution. A fixed Jira bug is not necessarily an alert that should have triggered an on-call page. The inputs also change from telemetry to text, and the paper does not explain what features the standard classifiers use for Jira. Because both the task and the input change, the result shows a difference between datasets, not clear evidence of transfer failure.

"MAXCONF(R)" is not defined in the paper. If it refers to the maximum RRF score from Equation 2, then comparing it with 0.5 is not meaningful, because the maximum possible score is only (3/61 $\approx$ 0.049). The paper should define "MAXCONF(R)" and explain its scale.

### Relevance

The work is relevant to incident management and software maintenance. Its focus on retrieving past resolutions is practical and complements work on root-cause analysis.

### Verifiability and Transparency

The code is accessible and contains configuration, tests, and reproduction instructions.

### Presentation

The paper is generally clear and well organized, and Figure 1 gives a helpful overview of ARISE. However, some important details are hard to follow, especially how confidence scores are calculated and how the reported cost savings are measured. These details should be explained more directly.

Questions for authors’ response
-------------------------------
1. Since both the relevance labels and ARISE's graph retriever use component overlap, how do you ensure that the evaluation does not favor ARISE? How do the other methods perform under the stricter relevance rule?
2. What does "MAXCONF(R)" measure, and how is its value and 0.5 threshold determined?
3. What inputs were used by the standard classifiers for the Jira AUPRC and ROC results, given that the corpus has no numeric telemetry?

Metareview
----------
Thank you for submitting your work to the ICSE research track and providing responses and clarifications.

On the positive side, all reviewers found the topic interesting, and highly timely given the growing reliance on coding agents. However, reviewers agreed that the paper is not ready for publication at ICSE 2027. The primary weakness identified was the lack of actionable implications beyond prior works analyzing agent context files broadly. Reviewers also pointed out several narrative shortcuts in the analysis that were not adequately addressed, as well as insufficient justification for focusing exclusively on not-to-do instructions rather than general rules.

We hope that the provided feedback will be useful, and we look forward to seeing an improved version of this paper in the future.

Recommendation
--------------
1. Reject
