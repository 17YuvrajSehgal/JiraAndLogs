We thank the reviewers for recognizing the problem's relevance and the value of budgeted reasoning. Their reviews point to a clear path: evaluate retrieved incidents' usefulness directly, sharpen our reading of the real-data results, and expand methodological detail. We request a major revision.
Reviewers A/B: Relevance labels and practical usefulness
The 0.970 Hit@5 on World of Logs (5,150 queries, 38,642 memory tickets) measures retrieval under an inferred relevance rule: same project and at least one shared Jira component. We will label it as such throughout, including the abstract, and distinguish it from diagnostic or resolution usefulness.
On overlap between labels and graph retrieval: the graph uses LLM-extracted entities from ticket text, not the Jira component field. Graph-only retrieval reaches 0.308 Hit@5 under the same rule, and removing it from fusion changes nothing significant. Under a stricter rule adding symptom overlap, hybrid retrieval reaches 0.74 Hit@5 (MRR 0.60) on the 4,362 of 5,150 queries retaining gold (Kafka 0.80, MariaDB 0.73). We will report all baselines under both definitions with query counts, and quantify missing component metadata and its effect on eligibility.
Our top priority is a blinded human evaluation of top-ranked results from ARISE and the strongest baselines. Raters will judge whether retrieved incidents provide useful diagnostic or resolution information, using hard same-project alternatives, a documented rubric, and inter-rater agreement. The existing Qwen2.5-7B assessment (300 labeled and 300 random pairs per dataset; 2.3-2.6/5 mean for labeled pairs) will be presented as exploratory.
Reviewers A/B: Real-data classification and paging
The telemetry-based classifiers expect numerical features World of Logs does not carry: all 94 feature columns were present but uniformly zero across 78,140 rows, so the classifiers could only produce constant predictions. We will remove the claim that their chance-level performance shows classification fails on real incidents, and replace this invalid comparison with text-based classifiers trained on ticket descriptions under the same held-out-project partition.
In the abstract and introduction we will replace statements that ticket-worthiness classification “does not transfer” with a narrower description of the text-based, resolution-label prediction task and its results. In RQ4 and the discussion we will replace the synthetic-versus-real classifier comparison with the text-based baselines, and state that the original zero-filled comparison cannot support a transfer conclusion.
ARISE’s own 0.506 triage accuracy is a separate result from retrieval-derived scores on text-only inputs, so the zero-fill explanation does not apply to it. It is also not evidence of working classification: the test split is 50.6% ticket-worthy, so a constant predictor scores 0.506, and ARISE does not exceed that. Part of the gap is our own label-space mismatch — the controller emits a third "borderline" decision for suppressed pages on 3,061 of 13,388 windows, which the binary gold cannot score correct. We will report the majority-class baseline, fix the mismatch, and state plainly that our triage on real incidents is also at chance. This reinforces rather than weakens our reframing: retrieval, not classification, is what transfers.
Jira resolution status and operational page-worthiness are different concepts. We will rename the real-data target “resolution-derived labels” throughout and remove claims that these labels measure real-world paging effectiveness. Assessing page-worthiness would require independently annotated operational urgency, which this corpus lacks.
Reviewer B: MAXCONF and thresholds
MAXCONF(R) is the maximum of available retrievers' triage_score values (default zero), separate from the fused RRF score; the 0.5 gate therefore does not apply to the raw RRF score, whose maximum the reviewer correctly computes as about 0.049. For the fine-tuned dense retriever this score comes from a logistic head over similarity features; hybrid retrieval computes it separately. We will specify features, training, range, and fallbacks per configuration in Section IV-A and Algorithm 1.
The 0.90 and 0.5 thresholds were fixed in advance, not tuned on test results. We will add sensitivity analyses for retrieval quality, escalation frequency, and cost, and align the algorithm, implementation, and evaluated controller behavior.
Reviewer B: Cost estimates
The 40–78% reductions are estimates from a per-skill cost model applied to controller decisions. We will correct the Table III caption and Section VII-C, which currently present the reduction as an observed quantity in wall-clock time and model usage. We will report per-step invocation and skip counts with denominators and cost assumptions and add measured wall-clock time and token usage for adaptive and run-all configurations, with offline training costs separate.
Reviewer C: Repeated runs and training
Section VII-A reports three-seed results for the telemetry datasets (0.632 ± 0.015 and 0.678 ± 0.004); we will extend World of Logs to multiple seeds. Retriever fine-tuning is offline and the model is frozen during evaluation. We will state this explicitly, add confidence intervals to Figure 4 separating query-level from training-run variation, and document fixed seeds and greedy decoding.
Reviewer C: Incident construction, labels, and presentation
Each memory item is a resolved ticket: a historical Jira issue (World of Logs) or a ticket generated for an injected fault (telemetry datasets). Synthetic labels come from fault injections; Jira classification labels from issue type and resolution; retrieval relevance from the project/component rule and its stricter variant. The LLM judge rates sampled pairs only and produces no benchmark labels; no human judgments are used. We will correct the annotation-kit statement in Section IX and release the human-study protocol.
We will provide the full judge prompt and sampling procedure, use one name for Hybrid-RRF, rewrite the flagged introduction passages, and clarify how continuous streams are grouped into decision windows and how timestamped memory reaches each decision.
Reviewer A: Related systems
We will compare FLASH, Triangle, DeepCT, and COMET by objectives, inputs, outputs, and evaluation settings, positioning ARISE's contribution: combining historical incident retrieval with capability-aware, budgeted evidence gathering. Where feasible we will add experimental comparisons and document adaptations.
Reviewer A: Artifacts
v1–v3 were internal names for progressively larger corpus variants. Experiments use v3; v2 is currently bundled. We will publish the exact v3 corpus on Zenodo with a matching version identifier, plus the manifest, query/memory partitions, preprocessing instructions, and configurations.
These revisions address the concerns about usefulness, comparison fairness, controller behavior, and reproducibility.
