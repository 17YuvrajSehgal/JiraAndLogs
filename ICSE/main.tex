\begin{abstract}
Automated incident triage is evaluated with striking uniformity: faults are
scripted into a demonstration microservice application, and a system is scored
on whether it can separate a faulty observation window from a healthy one.
Reported numbers are high and still improving. We ask whether that is the right
thing to measure.

We report a measurement study across three corpora of increasing realism: two
newly released datasets pairing multi-signal microservice telemetry with
incident tickets and per-window labels, and 38,642 real Apache Jira incidents
from 24 projects. We find that the dominant benchmark target is saturated by
construction and does not transfer --- ticket-worthiness classification reaches
ROC area 1.00 on injected faults but only 0.57--0.70 on real engineer-written
ticket text --- whereas incident-memory retrieval is measured identically on
both and does transfer.

We further find that evaluation choices routinely left implicit change not only
the magnitude of a result but its direction. Retrieval fusion is significantly
worse than a single dense retriever on a 347-item memory and significantly
better on a 38,642-item one. The training seed accounts for up to 28\% of
variance in a headline metric, and GPU fine-tuning does not reproduce at a fixed
seed. Moving between two defensible relevance relations changes Hit@5 by 0.23
and reorders the method table. Each of these reversed or materially altered one
of our own conclusions.

We conclude with a protocol for evaluating incident triage, each item tied to a
specific way our own results would have misled without it, and release the
datasets, code, and full result set.
\end{abstract}
